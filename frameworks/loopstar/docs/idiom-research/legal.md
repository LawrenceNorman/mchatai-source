# Music-Copyright Research for a Genre Idiom Library (LoopStar phrase packs)

Scope: US law primarily; EU/UK divergence flagged where material. Current as of Aug 2026. Not legal advice.

## 0. Executive summary

The plan — an original-in-idiom library of genre phrases (hooks, riffs, comping cells, bass lines) authored by studying the classic canon, stored as key-relative JSON and rendered by your own synth — is squarely inside a **50-year industry practice** (arranger keyboards, Band-in-a-Box, EZkeys MIDI packs) that has produced essentially **zero infringement suits against the vendors**, and post-2020 US case law (Led Zeppelin en banc, Katy Perry appeal, both Sheeran cases) has moved decisively toward *un*protecting the building blocks you want to use. The two danger zones are narrow and avoidable: (1) **verbatim transcription of a protectable melodic/bass phrase from an in-copyright song** (the one thing melody law clearly protects), and (2) **marketing copy that uses artist names/trademarks in a way implying endorsement**. Everything else — progressions, rhythm archetypes, grooves, bass *styles*, instrumentation, "the genre sound" — is idea/scène-à-faire territory.

## 1. Protectability by element (claims list)

### 1.1 Melodies and short hooks/riffs
- **CLAIM (SETTLED): Melody is the core protected element of a musical composition.** If any element of your packs can infringe, it is a copied melodic line. Substantial-similarity cases are overwhelmingly won or lost on melody.
- **CLAIM (SETTLED): There is no bright-line note-count below which a melody is automatically free.** The words-and-short-phrases rule (37 C.F.R. § 202.1) covers titles/slogans, not tunes. Protectability of a short figure turns on *originality*, not length.
- **CLAIM (SETTLED, 9th Cir.): A short ostinato built from commonplace elements is unprotectable.** *Gray v. Hudson (Perry)*, 28 F.4th 87 (9th Cir. 2022): the 8-note "Joyful Noise"/"Dark Horse" ostinato (a manifestly conventional scale-degree pattern, even pitch rhythm) was **not original enough for protection**; the $2.8M jury verdict was vacated on JMOL and the 9th Cir. affirmed. Key holding: a "combination of unprotectable elements" gets at most *thin* protection requiring virtually identical copying.
- **CLAIM (SETTLED): Very small takings from a composition are de minimis.** *Newton v. Diamond*, 388 F.3d 1189 (9th Cir. 2003): a 3-note, ~6-second flute figure was de minimis at the composition level.
- **CLAIM (LIKELY): A genuinely distinctive multi-bar riff/hook from an in-copyright song IS protectable and will be litigated as melody.** No case gives famous riffs a free pass; treat any recognizable hook (e.g., the "Superstition" clav riff, "Smoke on the Water") as protected expression. This is the one category your library must never transcribe verbatim.
- **Ed Sheeran outcomes (both SETTLED as judgments):**
  - *Griffin v. Sheeran* (S.D.N.Y. jury, May 2023): "Thinking Out Loud" did **not** infringe "Let's Get It On" — independent creation; the shared I–iii–IV–V progression + anticipated harmonic rhythm are "common building blocks."
  - *Structured Asset Sales v. Sheeran*, 120 F.4th 1066 (2d Cir., Nov. 1, 2024): affirmed dismissal **as a matter of law** — "the four-chord progression at issue—ubiquitous in pop music—even coupled with a syncopated harmonic rhythm, is too well-explored to meet the originality threshold." Also confirmed the 1909-Act **deposit-copy** rule: the copyright is defined by the filed sheet music, not the famous recording.
  - UK: *Sheeran v. Chokri* (EWHC, Apr. 2022): "Shape of You" cleared; UK requires proof of actual copying (conscious or subconscious) — similarity of a short phrase alone did not suffice.
- **Led Zeppelin (SETTLED, 9th Cir. en banc): *Skidmore v. Led Zeppelin*, 952 F.3d 1051 (2020):** "Stairway" verdict of non-infringement affirmed; the court (a) **abolished the inverse-ratio rule**, (b) held a descending chromatic line over common figures is a building block, (c) confined 1909-Act works to the deposit copy, (d) tightened selection-and-arrangement claims (must copy the *particular* arrangement, not scattered similar elements). District courts nationwide now rely on it to dismiss "borrowing" claims.

### 1.2 Chord progressions
- **CLAIM (SETTLED): Chord progressions as such are not copyrightable.** Treated as scènes à faire / common stock: standard rulings and the 2d Cir. in *SAS v. Sheeran* (2024) put this essentially beyond argument, even for a *specific* 4-chord loop *combined with* a syncopated harmonic rhythm. Your diatonic degree progressions `[1,6,2,5]` etc. are free to copy from any song, verbatim, at unlimited length of loop.
- **CLAIM (LIKELY): An extremely long, unusual through-composed harmonic sequence could contribute to a selection-and-arrangement claim** — theoretical; no modern plaintiff has won on harmony alone.

### 1.3 Bass lines
- **CLAIM (SETTLED as practice, UNTESTED as law): "Under Pressure"/"Ice Ice Baby" produced NO court ruling.** Queen/Bowie threatened suit; Vanilla Ice settled privately (credit + money; he later bought the publishing). It is industry folklore, not precedent. What it *does* show: a distinctive, recognizable note-for-note bass hook is treated commercially as protected melody.
- **CLAIM (LIKELY): A distinctive composed bass line is analyzed as a melodic line — protectable if original; a bass *style/archetype* (walking, root-fifth, 808 glide, ska skank, Motown-style syncopated eighths) is an unprotectable idiom/technique.** No case protects "walking bass" as such; conversely nothing immunizes copying the actual "Under Pressure" or "Good Times" line. Rule: archetypes free, transcriptions of named-song lines not.

### 1.4 Rhythm patterns / grooves / "feel"
- **CLAIM (SETTLED): Standard rhythms, drum patterns, and genre grooves are unprotectable scènes à faire.** (Standard rock beat, clave, four-on-the-floor, swung hats, dembow, etc.)
- **Blurred Lines (*Williams v. Gaye*, 895 F.3d 1106 (9th Cir. 2018)) — what it actually turned on (SETTLED as to the judgment, CONTESTED as to its reasoning):** The jury found infringement of "Got To Give It Up" based on an expert's "constellation" of eight similarities (signature phrase, hooks, bass melodies, keyboard parts, word painting) — *not* a holding that groove/feel is protectable. The 9th Cir. affirmed narrowly on **deference to the jury** and procedural posture, expressly deciding "on narrow grounds." Judge Nguyen's dissent — the majority let plaintiffs "copyright a musical style"/groove — is the most-cited part of the opinion.
- **CLAIM (LIKELY → approaching settled): Blurred Lines is an outlier that later courts have confined.** *Skidmore* (en banc, 2020) and *Gray* (2022) gutted the doctrinal moves that made it possible (inverse ratio, loose selection-and-arrangement); commentators and subsequent district courts treat groove/feel claims as non-starters. It was never formally overruled — so a jury-risk tail remains for "vibe" claims, which is a reason to keep your groove content archetypal rather than a bar-for-bar lift of one song's full multi-part texture.

### 1.5 Genre / style emulation ("in the style of X")
- **CLAIM (SETTLED): Musical style, genre, and idiom are not copyrightable** — idea/expression dichotomy plus scènes à faire. "No one can copyright a musical genre" (ABA 2025 review in the gen-AI context reaffirms this). Writing *new* material "in the style of Motown/trap/boom-bap" infringes nothing under copyright.
- **CLAIM (SETTLED): A performer's distinctive *voice/persona* is protected by state right of publicity and Lanham Act false endorsement, not copyright** — *Midler v. Ford*, 849 F.2d 460 (9th Cir. 1988); *Waits v. Frito-Lay*, 978 F.2d 1093 (9th Cir. 1992). Both were deliberate sound-alike *vocal imitations in advertisements*. Instrumental genre emulation inside a creative tool is far from these facts; the exposure is in *marketing that trades on an artist's name*, not in the notes.

### 1.6 EU/UK divergence (flag where big)
- **Originality standard**: EU/UK use "author's own intellectual creation" (*Infopaq*); no US-style scènes à faire doctrine, and a *short but distinctive* phrase can be a protected "substantial part" qualitatively. Slightly more protective of short hooks than post-*Gray* US law. (LIKELY)
- **Sampling**: *Pelham I* (CJEU C-476/17, 2019, Kraftwerk "Metall auf Metall"): even a ~2-second **recording** sample implicates the phonogram right unless modified to be unrecognizable — no de minimis. *Pelham II* (CJEU, Apr. 2026) softened this via a clarified **pastiche** exception for recognizable artistic reference. Irrelevant to you if you never ship audio lifted from records. (SETTLED in EU)
- **Terms**: EU/UK = life + 70. Much US-PD repertoire is **not** PD in Europe (e.g., "I Got Rhythm": George Gershwin d.1937 PD, but Ira's lyrics — d.1983 — are not). If the app ships worldwide with PD *melodies*, prefer composers dead >70 years, or note per-territory status. (SETTLED)

## 2. Composition rights vs sound-recording rights
- Two independent copyrights exist in a recorded song: the **musical work** (notes/lyrics — PA registration) and the **sound recording** (the fixed performance — SR registration, 17 U.S.C. § 102(a)(2),(7)). (SETTLED)
- **17 U.S.C. § 114(b)** expressly limits the sound-recording right: it "do[es] not extend to the making … of another sound recording that consists entirely of an independent fixation of other sounds, even though such sounds imitate or simulate those in the copyrighted sound recording." Sound-alikes are lawful *as to the recording*. (SETTLED — statutory text)
- **Consequence for LoopStar**: an engine that renders audio from its **own note data** through its **own/CC0-licensed synths and SoundFonts** can never infringe any third-party sound recording. The only third-party right that can ever be implicated is the **composition** — i.e., whether the note data itself copies protectable expression. Sampling case law (*Bridgeport* 6th Cir. 2005 — no de minimis for recording samples; *VMG Salsoul v. Ciccone*, 824 F.3d 871 (9th Cir. 2016) — de minimis OK; circuit split) is entirely inapplicable to you. (SETTLED)
- Corollary: your **SoundFont/sample-library inputs** are themselves sound recordings — keep provenance (CC0/licensed) records; that is a contract/EULA question, not a composition question.

## 3. Public domain in the US as of 2026
- **Compositions: everything published in 1930 or earlier is PD** (95-year term; 1930 works entered PD Jan. 1, 2026). 1931 works arrive Jan. 1, 2027. (SETTLED)
- **Sound recordings (Music Modernization Act schedule): recordings published in 1925 or earlier are PD** as of Jan. 1, 2026 (pre-1923 entered 2022; 1923–1946 recordings get 100 years; 1947–1956 get 110; 1957–2/15/1972 protected until 2067). (SETTLED)
- Unpublished works: life + 70. Trad/anonymous folk melodies with no identifiable publication were effectively never in copyright — but the *collected/arranged versions* you find are.
- **What this opens up for phrase packs**: the entire classical canon; ragtime (all Joplin); early blues (pre-1931 W.C. Handy, Delta/country blues sheet publications); 1920s jazz repertoire (stomps, early standards); Tin Pan Alley through 1930 — 1930 alone added "I Got Rhythm," "Georgia on My Mind," "Dream a Little Dream of Me" (US only; see EU caveat above); plus trad folk, hymns, work songs.
- **THE ARRANGEMENT TRAP (SETTLED):** A PD melody is free, but a modern **arrangement/edition** of it is a derivative work with its own copyright in the *new original material* (Copyright Office Circular 14). A 2010 jazz reharmonization of a 1908 tune, a modern hymnal harmonization, a cleaned-up "urtext-plus-editorial" edition — the additions are protected. Rules: (a) source from period sheet music (pre-1931 imprints, IMSLP first editions); (b) or extract only the raw PD melody/changes and re-voice yourself; (c) trivially conventional additions get no protection, but don't rely on that at scale. Note US has no UK-style typographical-arrangement right, so re-engraving PD notes is fine; it's *added musical content* that bites.

## 4. Prior-art legal posture (why the industry ships this safely)
- **Arranger keyboards (Yamaha/Korg/Roland style banks) & Band-in-a-Box**: ship **originally-composed accompaniment patterns in named idioms** — comping figures, bass patterns, drum grooves per style ("bossa," "60s soul," "country swing") — authored in-house by session musicians, *not* transcriptions of specific songs. Decades on the market; no US infringement suits located against the pattern content. The notable litigation runs the **other way**: *Yamaha v. Deli Electronics* (China, 2008) — Yamaha successfully sued keyboard clone-makers for copying *its* style data, establishing style banks as protectable content **owned by the pack author**. Third-party style vendors (e.g., Norton Music) sell "in the style of [hit song]"-indexed styles for BiaB and assert their own copyright in them; they use punning/coded song references and disclaimers rather than straight titles in product names.
- **Toontrack EZkeys/EZdrummer MIDI packs**: marketed as "inspired by" genres and eras ("20 songs inspired by hook-heavy EDM"), never as transcriptions; content is original performances by hired players; EULA permits use in user compositions, forbids redistribution of the MIDI as-is. No artist names on packs.
- **Scaler / Captain Chords-type plugins**: ship chord *progressions* and genre presets — pure unprotectable-building-block content (see § 1.2), which is why "chords of famous songs" features are legally trivial.
- **Hooktheory/TheoryTab** (the different case — it hosts actual *transcriptions* of in-copyright melodies+chords): its model is (a) a **self-imposed fair-use posture** — analyses limited to ≤30 seconds or 10% of the song, educational/analytical framing, no redundant sections; (b) **DMCA safe-harbor** compliance with a takedown policy and repeat-infringer termination; (c) TOS banning bulk export of the database. Occasional takedowns hit the *embedded YouTube videos*, not the note data. Its posture is **untested in court and is a fair-use claim, not settled law** — relevant to you only if you ever host user-submitted transcriptions of copyrighted songs. Your planned library (original phrases + PD transcriptions) does not need this crutch.
- Net: what these vendors actually ship is **original phrases in idiom**, plus verbatim *progressions* — exactly the safe zone. The absence of suits over 40+ years, against deep-pocketed defendants (Yamaha!), is strong practical evidence that idiom libraries are not a litigation target.

## 5. Practical design rules — checklist for the genre phrase library

**A. May be copied verbatim from any source, including in-copyright classics (SETTLED-safe):**
1. Chord progressions, at degree level or absolute — any length loop, incl. a specific song's changes (`[1,6,2,5]`, 12-bar blues, "Axis" I–V–vi–IV, rhythm changes).
2. Harmonic-rhythm placements (anticipations, push chords) — even combined with the progression (*SAS v. Sheeran*).
3. Rhythm archetypes: drum grooves, clave/skank/dembow/boom-bap patterns, comping-rhythm cells (beat-position lists like `[0,1.5,2,3.5]`), swing ratios, standard bass *figurations* (walking, root-fifth, octave 808 glides).
4. Voicing techniques: drop-2, rootless, shell, added 9ths, inversions — technique is unprotectable.
5. Instrumentation/GM-program choices, tempo ranges, densities, structural forms (AABA, verse-chorus, 12-bar).
6. **PD melodies and bass lines**: any composition published ≤1930 (US), transcribed from a period edition — usable note-for-note, including as hooks. (Add a `pdSource` field: title, year, edition.)

**B. Must be ORIGINAL-IN-IDIOM (never verbatim from in-copyright songs):**
7. Melodic hooks, riffs, ostinatos, fills, and lead lines: author new phrases that use the genre's *vocabulary* (scale choices, blue notes, cell shapes, contour types, call-response) — study the canon, then write fresh cells. Two-to-eight-note commonplace figures are individually unprotectable (*Gray*), but the pack policy should be "no transcribed hooks" regardless — it's cheap and eliminates the only real melody risk.
8. Named-song bass lines ("Under Pressure," "Good Times," "Billie Jean"): treat as melodies — write *style-alike* lines (same technique: syncopated root-octave disco eighths), never the actual note sequence.
9. Full multi-part textures: don't clone one specific song's entire simultaneous arrangement (groove + bass + keys + hook together) even from archetypes — that's the residual Blurred Lines "constellation" tail. Recombine cells across sources instead; your recombination engine does this naturally.
10. Test for 7–9: could a musicologist point to one specific in-copyright song this phrase is *transcribed from*? If yes, rewrite. "Sounds like the genre" is the goal; "is recognizably that song" is the bug.

**C. Provenance & process hygiene:**
11. Keep authorship records per phrase: author, date, `influences` (songs studied), `pdSource` where applicable. Original phrases in your packs are **your** copyrightable content (the Yamaha-China lesson) — license the repo accordingly (content license distinct from code).
12. For PD material, transcribe from pre-1931 sheet music, not from modern fake books, hymnals, or engraved editions (arrangement trap, § 3).
13. If users can ever submit phrases: adopt the Hooktheory kit — TOS, DMCA agent + takedown flow, repeat-infringer policy.

**D. Naming, metadata, and marketing (trademark / right of publicity):**
14. Genre and era descriptors are free: "soul," "boom bap," "'60s Detroit soul feel," "'70s funk," "two-tone ska." Avoid **"Motown"** in user-facing pack names/marketing — it's a live trademark (UMG); "Motown feel" in a hidden metadata field is low-risk but "Detroit soul" costs nothing. Same for "EDM-brand" labels owned by companies.
15. Song titles: **titles are not copyrightable** (37 C.F.R. § 202.1), so an `influences: ["Ain't No Sunshine"]` metadata list is fine. Keep such references descriptive and out of product names/ads.
16. Artist names: truthful reference is protected **nominative fair use** (use only the name, minimum necessary, nothing implying endorsement — *New Kids on the Block* factors), but false-endorsement (§ 43(a) Lanham) and right-of-publicity risk (*Midler*, *Waits*) rises the closer a name gets to *advertising*. Rules: never "James Brown Pack"; avoid artist names in UI/pack titles and store copy; "influences" metadata or docs saying "studied from classic recordings by …" is defensible; the industry norm (Toontrack) is to omit names entirely — follow it for anything user-facing.
17. Never imply the artist made, approved, or is featured in the content; no artist likenesses/photos.

**E. Renderer-side (already satisfied):**
18. All audio from own synthesis + CC0/licensed SoundFonts → no sound-recording exposure ever (§ 114(b)). Keep SoundFont license provenance in the instrument catalog.

**Bottom line risk ranking:** verbatim in-copyright hooks/bass lines = the only red zone; single-song full-texture clones = amber (Blurred Lines tail); everything else in the plan = green under current, post-2020 US law, and green in EU/UK too so long as melodies are original or PD-in-territory.
